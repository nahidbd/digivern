Glossary

Key terms in digital government and digital public infrastructure, in plain language.

Digital public infrastructure (DPI)
Shared, society-wide digital systems such as identity, payments and data exchange that public and private services can build on. They are usually governed to be interoperable, inclusive and safe.
Digital public goods (DPGs)
Open-source software, open data, open AI models, open standards and open content that follow privacy and other laws and are designed to do no harm.
Foundational ID
A general-purpose identity system, usually national, that registers the whole population and can be used across many services.
Functional ID
An identity credential issued for one purpose or sector, such as a voter card, tax number or health insurance card.
CRVS
Civil registration and vital statistics: the continuous recording of births, deaths, marriages and other life events, and the statistics produced from those records.
Data exchange layer
Technology and rules that let separate government databases share data securely through standard interfaces instead of point-to-point connections.
Interoperability
The ability of different systems and organisations to exchange information and use it, covering technical, semantic, organisational and legal aspects.
Consent management
Processes and tools that record what a person agreed to share, with whom and for what purpose, and let them review or withdraw that agreement.
G2P payments
Government-to-person payments, such as wages, pensions and social transfers, increasingly made into accounts or mobile wallets.
Once-only principle
Citizens and businesses provide a piece of information to government only once; agencies then reuse it, with safeguards, instead of asking again.
AI impact assessment
A structured review before deploying an AI system that examines its purpose, risks to people, data use and the controls needed.
Agentic AI
AI systems that can plan and take actions across tools or services with limited human input, rather than only answering questions.
Zero trust
A security approach where no user or device is trusted by default, even inside the network; every request is verified.
Open data
Data that anyone can access, use and share, published in machine-readable formats under an open licence.
Digital sovereignty
A state’s ability to make independent decisions about its digital infrastructure, data and technology suppliers.
E-waste
Discarded electrical and electronic equipment. It contains valuable materials and hazardous substances, so it needs safe collection and recycling.
