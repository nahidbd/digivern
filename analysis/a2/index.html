A health card for 200 million people: what Bangladesh can learn from India, the UK and Singapore

2 Nov 2026 · Updated 9 Nov 2026 · 14 min read · 21 sources

Bangladesh has promised every citizen an e-health card by 2028, linked to a national health database, and has doubled its health budget. Other countries show that the card is the easy part. Linking records, getting hospitals to use them, paying for it and protecting the data take years.

Key numbers

  • ~200 m people the planned Bangladesh health database would cover1
  • 2028 target for an e-health card for every Bangladeshi citizen2
  • ~79% share of Bangladesh’s health spending paid out of pocket12
  • 93 cr+ ABHA health accounts in India, linked to 104 crore records4
  • 16.7 m people used England’s NHS App in September 20265

The short version

On 18 October 2026, State Minister for Health Dr M A Muhit set out plans for a universal e-health card and a national health database for nearly 200 million people, linking consultations, prescriptions, medicine purchases and test results 1. The government’s stated target is a card for every citizen by 2028 2, starting in five districts: Khulna, Noakhali, Bogura, Sirajganj and Narsingdi 3. The health budget for 2026–27 nearly doubled to Tk 69,409 crore, crossing 1% of GDP for the first time 11, but no budget line or rollout schedule for the card has been published 111. A revised project proposal now before the Planning Commission puts the pilot at Tk 493.88 crore, nearly three times the earlier Tk 168.16 crore, and adds a sixth district, Jhalakathi, for July 2026 to December 2028 21.

What others did

India built a health ID first and records later. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission was approved in February 2022 with Rs 1,600 crore for five years 16; it now has more than 93 crore ABHA accounts and over 104 crore linked health records, and its “scan and share” queue service issued over 23 crore tokens and cut hospital registration waits from about an hour to a few minutes 4. The scale came from linking the ID to services people already used.

England took the opposite route: a single app on top of existing records. The NHS App had 16.7 million users in September 2026, up 24% in a year, and 6.3 million people used it to order repeat prescriptions that month 5. A single patient record joining hospital, GP and social care data is due in the app by 2028 under the NHS Modernisation Bill, announced on 11 May 2026 617.

Singapore and Thailand are cutting app sprawl. Singapore is merging four public health apps into an enhanced HealthHub app by November 2026, with the old apps switched off by February 2027 7. Thailand announced in 2025 that its Mor Prom+ “super app” would replace more than 50 separate government health apps 8. In Africa, AU member states validated a continental health data governance framework in October 2026 9.

Where Bangladesh stands

Bangladesh is not starting from zero. The Directorate General of Health Services piloted a Shared Health Record on the open-source OpenHIE and OpenMRS (Bahmni) stack in Kaliganj from November 2015 and in Gazipur district from 2016 10. The Bangladesh Digital Health Strategy 2023–2027 already calls for a health ID linked to the national ID, a health information exchange and electronic health records in all public facilities by 2027 15. What has been missing is a single identifier used across public and private providers, and the instruction to use it. The e-health card can supply the identifier; the five-district rollout is meant to link cardholders to electronic referral and patient management systems 3.

The money context matters. About 79% of health spending in Bangladesh is paid by households out of pocket 12, so anything that cuts repeat tests, lost records and unnecessary visits has a direct value for families. But the health ministry has a weak record of spending its development budget, which experts say could limit the gains from the larger allocation 1112.

GovTec’s assessment

The international evidence points to four conditions. First, make the record useful on day one: India’s scan-and-share and England’s prescription ordering drove use because they saved patients time 45. Second, build on what exists: the Shared Health Record pilots, the digital health strategy and the national ID give Bangladesh a head start that a card-first approach could waste 1015. Third, link the card to money: as health researchers argue, a card that only stores records changes little for a family paying most costs out of pocket, while a card linked to cashless care or insurance changes behaviour 1213. Fourth, protect the data before it is pooled: a database of 200 million health records is a high-value target, as Moldova’s October 2026 e-government breach showed for linked registries 20. A published budget, a rollout timetable and clear access rules under the 2025 data protection ordinance 19 would turn the 2028 promise into a plan.

Money and value

Funding. Bangladesh’s 2026–27 health budget of Tk 69,409 crore includes a Tk 23,522 crore block allocation in the development budget 11, which could fund the card, but no specific allocation has been published 1114. The card is to be delivered through a technical assistance project 2. A revised proposal before the Planning Commission puts the pilot at Tk 493.88 crore: Tk 328.73 crore from the government and an ADB loan of Tk 165.15 crore, to equip 881 facilities and 9,040 field workers in six districts; DGHS says it would reach 1.64 crore citizens 21. That is about Tk 301 per targeted citizen (GovTec calculation). The cost nearly tripled from Tk 168.16 crore when the pilot grew from Khulna alone to six districts, and the Planning Commission has asked for the card’s unit cost, distribution cost and sustainability 21.

Benchmarks. India budgeted Rs 1,600 crore over five years for its digital health mission 16 and has created more than 93 crore health accounts 4, roughly Rs 17 per account (GovTec calculation). England is pairing its single patient record with digital advice to cut into £14 billion a year of outpatient costs 17. Both show that the core digital layer is cheap compared with the health spending it can make more efficient.

Who pays and who gains. With about 79% of spending out of pocket 12, households bear most of the cost of duplicate tests and lost records today, so they would gain most from a working shared record. Researchers argue the card delivers its real economic value only if it is linked to cashless or insured care for poor families 13. The main fiscal risk is under-spending: experts warn that the ministry’s weak record in using its development budget, and corruption and delays in hospitals, could leave the money unspent or wasted 1112.

Voices

  • Prof Foara Tasmim, Additional Director General (Planning and Development), DGHS (Government, support): Said the cost rose because the pilot grew from one district to six, “not a higher budget for any single component”.21
  • Dr M A Muhit, State Minister for Health and Family Welfare (Government, support): Unveiled a universal e-health card and a national database linking consultations, prescriptions, medicine purchases and tests.1
  • Sardar Md Sakhawat Husain, Health and Family Welfare Minister (Government, support): Told Parliament the target is e-health cards for all citizens by 2028 through a technical assistance project.2
  • Dr Imrul Mohammad Sifat, Bangladesh Health Watch and BRAC JPGSPH (Expert, mixed): The card should not be a “digital flash drive for medical records” but should guarantee cashless care for poor families.13
  • Prof Shafiun Nahin Shimul, Institute of Health Economics, Dhaka University (Expert, concern): Warns the health ministry’s poor record of spending its development budget could limit gains from the bigger allocation.11
  • Prof Syed Abdul Hamid, Institute of Health Economics, Dhaka University (Expert, concern): More money will not improve outcomes without tackling corruption, political interference and bureaucratic delays.12
  • Pratik Ejaz, President, Bangladesh Health Reporters Forum (Civil society, concern): Administrative inefficiency means significant health funds go unspent each year; calls for better planning capacity.12
  • Farjana Yesmin, researcher in trustworthy AI for healthcare (Expert, mixed): Build governance first: impact assessments and testing of any AI linked to the card on Bangladeshi patients, in Bangla.14
  • Dr Jean Kaseya, Director-General, Africa CDC (Development partner, support): “Health sovereignty begins with owning, protecting and using our own data.”18
  • Karin Smyth, UK health minister (Government, support): Says England’s single patient record will give patients “real control” over their care.17

What to watch

  • Approval of the revised six-district proposal (Tk 493.88 crore, part-financed by an ADB loan) by the Planning Commission and ECNEC 21.
  • Whether the card is linked to cashless or insured care for poor families, as researchers propose 13.
  • Whether private hospitals, diagnostic centres and pharmacies are required to connect.
  • Rules on who can access records under the Personal Data Protection Ordinance 2025 19.
  • How much of the 2026–27 health development budget is actually spent by June 2027 11.
