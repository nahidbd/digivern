When the front door goes dark: what Poland’s ePUAP attack says about securing shared e-government platforms

16 Nov 2026 · 16 min read · 52 sources

On 10 November Poland detected a cyberattack on ePUAP, the platform behind official e-mailboxes and Trusted Profile signatures, and took it offline as a precaution. Four days later it was still down, and the minister said the attackers had been preparing to paralyse public administration in December. What the incident shows about single shared platforms, how Poland’s laws and money stack up, and what Albania, Costa Rica, the Netherlands, Belgium, Moldova, Estonia and Ukraine learned.

Key numbers

  • 3.5+ days ePUAP mailboxes unavailable, 10 Nov evening to at least 14 Nov morning (GovTec calculation)12
  • 272,941 incidents handled by Poland’s national CSIRTs in 2025, up 144.4% on 202428
  • 13.5 m mObywatel users in October 2026; 16 m Trusted Profile users in 202318
  • ~4 bn zł total yearly cybersecurity spending; over 3.1 bn zł of it civilian (2025)30
  • 38.2% share of EU cyber incidents that targeted public administration, Jul 2024–Jun 202537
  • 98% availability ePUAP’s operator was contracted to deliver — up to 7.3 days of downtime a year15

The short version

Shortly before 21:00 on Tuesday 10 November 2026, Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Digital Affairs Krzysztof Gawkowski announced that a cyberattack on ePUAP, Poland’s Electronic Platform of Public Administration Services, had been “detected and stopped” 123. The platform’s electronic mailboxes became unavailable to citizens and public offices, and users trying to log in saw a maintenance notice 210. The ministry’s communications director, Marek Gieorgica, said: “We simply disabled it as a precautionary measure,” adding that the system had not been compromised or disrupted by the attack 1.

Two days later Gawkowski said the attack had come from abroad and that logs showed preparation for a coordinated operation meant to paralyse government administration in December — “some kind of administrative bomb” 79. The Internal Security Agency (ABW) is investigating 7. On 14 November ePUAP was still unavailable, and gov.pl warned that the Trusted Profile (Profil Zaufany), the national login node and signature services might be unavailable from 18:00 that day to 07:00 on 15 November 1213. By the morning of 16 November GovTec had found no announcement that ePUAP was fully restored.

The lesson reaches beyond Poland. Login, signature and official mail are shared “front door” services: when they stop, many unrelated services stop with them. Europe’s cyber agency counts public administration as the most targeted sector in the EU, with 38.2% of incidents 37. Poland has new law and record budgets; what the ePUAP episode tests is whether it has enough redundancy, fallback channels and openness when the front door has to be shut.

What is known — and what is not

Known: the attack was detected on the evening of 10 November 2; the platform was taken offline as a precaution, not because it failed 14; the Central IT Centre (Centralny Ośrodek Informatyki, COI), which runs ePUAP, says its infrastructure is secure 211; ABW was brought in under standard procedures 7; and full restoration will come only after the COI team confirms there is no threat, with no date given 413.

Not known: how the attack was carried out, who was behind it, and whether attackers reached any data 111. Specialist outlets suggested a denial-of-service (DDoS) attack 4, but the minister’s description of logs showing “preparatory activity” for a later operation 7 points to something more than a flood of traffic. The two official messages — “not compromised” 1 and “preparation for a specific time” 7 — have not yet been reconciled publicly. Gieorgica also said ePUAP does not collect information about citizens 6.

The context is a run of incidents. A week or two earlier the ministry had preventively shut some Public Information Bulletin (BIP) sites after a vulnerability was found in PHP code 6. In late 2025 attackers tried to disrupt the power grid, which Gawkowski said pointed to Russian sabotage 33. In August 2026 data on almost 19 million people was taken from the medical platform MyDr, which Gawkowski called one of the largest incidents in Poland’s history 34.

Background

ePUAP was conceived in 2001 as a flagship way for citizens to contact offices 16. For years it struggled: upkeep cost up to about 19 million zł a year, and its improved version, ePUAP2, cost almost 50 million zł 16. The Trusted Profile — a free online identity used to log in and sign documents — took off only later: from nearly 655,000 profiles in 2016 to over 7.2 million in the first half of 2020 15, over 10 million in March 2021 16 and 16 million in April 2023 17. The mObywatel app, which shares the same identity layer, had 13.5 million users in October 2026 18, and users logged in an average of 1.5 million times a day in 2025 19.

Outages are not new. Spider’s Web counted failures before a corporate filing deadline in March 2019, a nationwide outage of ePUAP, the Trusted Profile and the PESEL register on 22 January 2020, and Trusted Profile failures when COVID-19 vaccination booking opened in April 2021 16. In June 2023 the then minister reported “another DDoS attack” on ePUAP, with services available but disrupted 35. The Supreme Audit Office (NIK) found in 2021 that ePUAP was often unstable, that contracts set availability at only 98% for ePUAP and 99% for the Trusted Profile, and that the operator had not fully implemented ISO/IEC 27001 or full security tests 15.

Since 2025 ePUAP has been shrinking in role. The Act on Electronic Delivery of 18 November 2020 made e-Doręczenia (e-delivery) the legal channel, phased in from 1 January 2025 22. At the end of 2025 ePUAP lost its equivalence with e-delivery, so general letters moved to e-Doręczenia 21. ePUAP is still needed for correspondence between offices and with administrative courts 5. That is why the outage hit offices hardest — and why it is still serious even though citizens now have another route.

What other countries did

Albania (2022) is the warning about dwell time. Iranian state actors were inside the Albanian government network for about 14 months before ransomware and wiper attacks made websites and services unavailable, according to the US agencies CISA and FBI 38. After the attack of 15 July 2022 Albania cut diplomatic ties with Iran 39. Gawkowski’s description of logs showing groundwork for a later strike 7 echoes that pattern.

Costa Rica (2022) is the warning about cost. The Conti ransomware group hit the Finance Ministry and other agencies, taking customs and tax platforms down; the new president declared a national emergency, and an exporters’ group estimated about US$200 million in losses 40. The Netherlands (2025) shows that a single login is a single target: a large DDoS attack left DigiD, the national login used by the tax office, municipalities and benefit agencies, unreachable for more than five hours 41. Belgium (October 2026) took its MyMinfin tax portal offline as a precaution just before a 31 October filing deadline and promised extra time to file 4243.

Moldova (October 2026) shows what attackers do with interconnected systems: they exploited services that needed no login, then pulled data tied to about 750,000 identification numbers from linked registers; the government disconnected more than 30 systems 44. Estonia built for the worst case: a “data embassy” in a Tier 4 data centre in Luxembourg holds backups and can run the most critical services 45, and its X-Road exchange layer signs and encrypts outgoing data and logs incoming data 46. Ukraine moved state registers to the cloud in the first days of the 2022 invasion; minister Mykhailo Fedorov said “Russian missiles can’t destroy the cloud” 47. Even so, a December 2024 attack forced the Justice Ministry to suspend state registers and disrupted five Diia services, with recovery expected to take up to two weeks 48.

Where Poland stands

Law: the amended National Cybersecurity System Act, which transposes the EU’s NIS2 directive, was passed on 23 January 2026, signed on 19 February, published on 2 March and entered into force on 3 April 2026 23. It covers public entities, lets the minister designate high-risk ICT suppliers, and sets fines of up to €10 million or 2% of revenue for key entities — most of them only from 3 April 2028 23. Entities have 6 months to register and 12 months to put risk-management measures in place 24. A new Cybersecurity Strategy to 2029 was adopted on 10 March 2026 25. Under the EU’s eIDAS 2.0 regulation, in force since 20 May 2024, Poland must offer a European Digital Identity Wallet by the end of 2026 26; the ministry plans an interim mObywatel Europa app by then 18.

Threat level: national CSIRTs handled 272,941 incidents in 2025, up 144.4%; CSIRT NASK alone handled 260,783 28. In 2024 the total was 111.66 thousand, and Gawkowski said Russia and Belarus were behind the largest number of attacks 27; he has also called Poland the most attacked country in Europe 30. CERT Polska, the NASK team founded in 1996, described 2024 as a record year on practically all its statistics 51, and its 2025 report has a chapter on observed APT group activity 52. The ABW’s CSIRT GOV, which covers government, received over 23,000 reports in 2025 and classified about 5,000 as incidents, 13 of them serious; it points to sustained activity by Russia- and Belarus-linked groups, including DDoS 29.

Money: Poland spends about 4 billion zł a year on cybersecurity, over 3.1 billion of it civilian 30. The 2025 civilian plan included 355 million zł from the Cybersecurity Fund, 860 million zł from the National Recovery Plan (KPO) including the “Cyberbezpieczny Rząd” programme, and 1.8 billion zł from the European Funds for Digital Development, including “Cyberbezpieczny Samorząd” 31. That local-government programme is worth 1.5 billion zł, with up to 850,000 zł per unit, for over 2,000 local governments 32.

Gaps: in early February 2026 more than half of the public entities obliged to have an e-delivery address still had none 5. NIK’s 2021 audit found 98% availability in the ePUAP contract and recommended about 99.9% 15; there is no public evidence the target has since been raised. The ministry has promised updates 4 but had not, by the time of writing, said what happened or how the platform was hardened.

GovTec’s assessment

GovTec’s view: taking ePUAP offline was defensible. When logs suggest attackers are preparing a later strike, as in Albania 387, shutting the door and checking the house is safer than keeping it open. Belgium did the same with its tax portal 42. The cost of caution is measurable: about 3.5 days of unavailability by 14 November — roughly half of the 7.3 days a year the ePUAP contract allowed, and about nine times what a 99.9% target would allow (GovTec calculation) 1512.

The bigger issue is architecture. ePUAP, the Trusted Profile, the national node and signature services are linked, so maintenance on one touches patient accounts and logins elsewhere 14. Poland’s move to e-Doręczenia has spread risk, and citizens had another channel this time 21. But offices and administrative courts still rely on ePUAP 5, and we found no public fallback plan for them during the outage. Estonia’s data embassy 45 and Ukraine’s cloud backups 47 show that “where do we run if the primary site is out?” needs an answer before an incident.

Communication is the third weakness. Saying both that the system was not compromised 1 and that attackers prepared an “administrative bomb” 7 confuses the public. Poland has the legal tools (NIS2), the incident teams and record budgets 232830. What it lacks is a published standard for how long a shared front-door service may be down, and a post-incident report. We would add three things: a 99.9% availability target with independent audit, as NIK proposed 15; a tested fallback for inter-office and court correspondence; and a public post-incident report within weeks, not months. About 85% of the itemised 2025 civilian cyber plan comes from EU programmes (GovTec calculation) 31; resilience for core platforms should also be paid for from the regular national budget.

Money and value

Who pays: the 2025 civilian cybersecurity plan of over 3.1 billion zł was built from targeted grants (90 million zł), the national CSIRT at NASK (40 million zł), the Cybersecurity Fund (355 million zł), the National Recovery Plan (860 million zł) and the European Funds for Digital Development (1.8 billion zł) 31. The two EU-funded lines make up about 2.66 billion zł, or roughly 85% of the 3.15 billion zł itemised (GovTec calculation) 31. In 2024 the Cybersecurity Fund signed 112 support agreements worth 331.5 million zł 27.

What platforms cost: ePUAP’s upkeep was up to about 19 million zł a year and ePUAP2 cost almost 50 million zł, according to a 2021 review 16; NIK found that an annex to the ePUAP and Trusted Profile contracts raised the operator’s maximum pay by nearly 70 million zł, without the ministry verifying the cost calculations 15. These are small sums next to the national cyber budget, which suggests that higher availability standards are affordable.

What outages cost: Poland has not published an estimate for the ePUAP outage. Elsewhere the bill was large: Costa Rican exporters estimated about US$200 million in losses from the 2022 ransomware bottlenecks 40. Belgium extended tax deadlines after its October 2026 portal shutdown 43 — a cost borne in delayed revenue and administrative work rather than cash.

Voices

  • Krzysztof Gawkowski, Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Digital Affairs (Government, support): Says the attack came from abroad and was preparation for “some kind of administrative bomb” in December; citizens are not at risk but the threat was large.7
  • Marek Gieorgica, communications director, Ministry of Digital Affairs (Government, support): “We simply disabled it as a precautionary measure”; says the system was not compromised and restoration waits for COI checks.1
  • Supreme Audit Office (NIK) (Government, concern): Found ePUAP frequently unstable, a too-narrow definition of critical incidents, and recommended availability of about 99.9% instead of 98%.15
  • Radosław Maćkiewicz, Director, Centralny Ośrodek Informatyki (COI) (Government, support): Says e-Doręczenia will be the main means of electronic communication and that an address is set up very quickly.21
  • Magdalena Gaj, President, Poczta Polska (Industry, support): Calls the hybrid e-delivery service a bridge between the digital state and citizens who still need paper.20
  • Check Point Software Technologies (Industry, concern): On earlier DDoS attacks on ePUAP in 2023, said the method was typical of pro-Russian hacker groups.36
  • Juhan Lepassaar, Executive Director, ENISA (Expert, concern): Warns that systems people rely on are “intertwined”, so a disruption in one area can spread through the supply chain.37
  • Instytut Sobieski (think tank) (Civil society, concern): Argues Poland responds to incidents reactively and needs a layered strategy of “resilience, not firefighting”.49
  • Marcin Wiącek, Commissioner for Human Rights, relaying citizens’ complaints (Users, concern): Questioned officials being required to use private Trusted Profile or mObywatel accounts for work tasks in e-Doręczenia, citing privacy.50

What to watch

  • When ePUAP is fully restored, and whether the ministry publishes what the attack was and how the platform was hardened 413.
  • Whether the attack the minister says was planned for December materialises, and whether ABW names a perpetrator 78.
  • Whether availability targets for ePUAP and the Trusted Profile are raised towards the 99.9% NIK recommended 15.
  • Registration and compliance figures under the NIS2 amendment of the KSC Act 2324.
  • Whether Poland meets the end-2026 deadline for a European Digital Identity Wallet 2618.
  • Progress on e-delivery addresses for public bodies and the draft amendment to the e-delivery law 520.
